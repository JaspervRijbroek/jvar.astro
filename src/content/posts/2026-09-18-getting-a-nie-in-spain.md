Moving to Spain comes with a little parade of admin, and opening a bank account in Spain is one of the first jobs that suddenly affects real life. Rent, utilities, deposits, day-to-day spending - it all gets easier once you have a local account. We did ours early on after arriving, with our son beside us and our Spanish still very much in the hopeful-smiling phase, and it was far more manageable than I’d built it up to be.

For us, the nudge was simple. We needed a Spanish account to get settled properly, and we needed it before all our paperwork had caught up. We started with Santander because it was one of the banks saying foreigners could open an account before getting an NIE. At that stage of a move, that is exactly the sort of sentence you cling to.

## Your residency status matters more than your city

The biggest difference is not whether you are in Madrid, Valencia or a village where everybody knows the bakery opening hours by heart. It is whether you are opening the account as a resident or a non-resident, and what documents match your status.

[Banco de España](https://clientebancario.bde.es/pcb/es/blog/cuales-son-los-tramites-para-abrir-una-cuenta-.html) explains that banks identify people differently depending on who they are. Spaniards use a DNI. EU citizens may be asked for a passport or national ID, plus an NIE. Non-EU foreigners may be asked for a TIE. If you are not resident in Spain yet, a bank may also want proof of non-resident status.

For most people, that means turning up with your passport or ID, and your NIE if you’ve already got one. If you do not have an NIE yet, it is still possible, but you need a bank that accepts that setup. Santander says foreign non-residents can open an account in Spain with a passport, and its pages for [foreign customers](https://www.bancosantander.es/extranjeros/) and the [basic payment account](https://www.bancosantander.es/particulares/cuentas-bancarias/cuenta-basica) also mention signing a tax-residence declaration if you are not resident in Spain.

That distinction is worth getting straight before you book anything. A resident account and a non-resident account are not the same thing, and banks do not all treat them the same way.

## How it went for us at Santander

We chose Santander because we needed something that could work before the NIE arrived. That part lined up well with what the bank says it offers, and for us it did the job.

The first hurdle was language. The branch staff spoke Spanish, we spoke patchy Spanish plus determination, and Google Translate ended up being the fourth person at the meeting. We went in with our passports, did the registration, and it was all fairly quick by Spanish banking standards. Our son spent part of the visit trying to work out whether the chair could be used as climbing equipment, which added just the right amount of suspense.

We did not leave with physical cards that day. We got access to the account first, which meant we could log in straight away. In our branch, we were then able to add the cards to Apple Pay or Google Pay before the physical cards arrived. Ours turned up by post about a week later.

I would treat that mobile-wallet bit as a happy branch-specific win, not something to count on everywhere. Card delivery times can vary, and I would ask before you leave how the card arrives, how you activate it, and whether you can use the account digitally first.

## If I were doing it again tomorrow

If a friend messaged me saying, we’ve just landed and need a Spanish bank account this week, this is the calm little plan I’d send over:

1. Work out whether you need a resident account, a non-resident account, or a basic payment account.
2. Gather your documents: passport or national ID, NIE or TIE if you have one, and any proof of non-resident or tax-residence status the bank asks for.
3. Choose your route: in-branch with a bank such as Santander, or online with a provider such as Revolut if you meet its residency rules.
4. Complete the identity check and sign the contract and any declarations.
5. Before you sign, ask what the account costs: monthly fee, card fee, transfer fee, and whether there are extra charges for a non-resident account.
6. Ask how and when you will get the card, and whether you can use the account before it arrives.

Written down, that sounds beautifully grown-up. Ours involved me pretending I had understood a banking term two minutes before I actually had, my partner catching the translation properly, and our little one asking for a snack at exactly the moment signatures became important. Still, one visit and it was done.

## Bring more paperwork than feels reasonable

This is one of those jobs where an overstuffed folder is a comfort.

Banco de España says a bank may ask not just for ID but also for documents linked to anti-money-laundering checks, including evidence of where your money comes from. It also warns that a bank can block access to funds if you cannot justify the source. If you are planning to transfer a larger amount from abroad for rent, deposits or the first stretch of family life, take that seriously.

So alongside your passport and NIE or TIE if you have one, I would be ready with any proof of address, proof of non-resident or tax-residence status, and source-of-funds paperwork that could help. You may not need all of it. We did not get asked for every possible document. But I would always rather carry one folder too many than do the sad little march back out to fetch another paper.

If I did our visit again, I would also save a handful of useful words in Spanish on my phone beforehand. Not a whole script, just the bits that matter: tax residence, fees, transfer from abroad, card delivery. Google Translate did heroic work for us, but a tiny bit of prep would have made me look less like a man trying to revise for an exam in the waiting area.

## Branch bank or online option?

For us, a branch made sense because our paperwork was still in motion and we wanted a bank that could handle that. Santander says foreigners can start the process from abroad or after arriving in Spain, which fits that early-moving stage quite well.

If getting to a branch is awkward, or the language side feels like one admin challenge too many, an online option may suit you better. Revolut says you can open an account online if you are 18 or over, live legally in a supported country, and provide a mobile number, email, personal details, a valid official ID, and a selfie through the app, as explained in its [getting started guide](https://help.revolut.com/es-ES/help/profile-and-plan/getting-started-with-revolut/) and page on [supported countries](https://help.revolut.com/es-ES/help/profile-and-plan/profile-plan/verifying-identity/what-countries-are-supported/). Revolut also says its standard account can be opened and used at no cost.

The important thing is that an online bank is not simply the same thing without the building. The eligibility rules are different, the setup is different, and the paperwork can be different too. If your status is a bit awkward, a traditional branch bank may be easier. If you meet the residency rules and want to do it from the sofa after bedtime, online can be wonderfully appealing.

## Fees, timing, and the one thing I would ask before signing

Costs vary by bank and by account. Banco de España explains the general process, but it does not set prices. Santander’s fees depend on the account type and its current tariff sheet. Revolut says its standard account can be opened and used free of charge, with paid plans adding extra features.

That means the useful question is not really, what does a bank account in Spain cost. It is, what will this exact account cost me. Before signing, I would ask four plain questions: is there a monthly fee, is there a card fee, what do transfers cost, and are there any extra charges because I am opening it as a non-resident. Those four cover most of the annoying surprises.

Timing varies too. Santander says opening can be done quickly, and that was close to our experience. We had account access set up fast, then the physical cards arrived by post about a week later. Yours may be faster or slower, so I would ask the branch rather than pinning your hopes on a fixed timeline.

For us, the nicest part was not the account itself. It was the tiny feeling of relief afterwards. Suddenly we could sort payments properly and stop feeling like we were improvising our way through the basics of everyday life in Spain.

So if this job is sitting on your list looking larger than it really is, I would not let it loom for too long. Pick the bank that fits your paperwork, bring the full folder, and go do it - even if your Spanish is held together by goodwill and a translation app. We managed it with a small child in tow, and you can too.
